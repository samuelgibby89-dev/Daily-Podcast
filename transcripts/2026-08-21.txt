Good morning. The story running underneath everything right now is the Treasury market, and it's not going away quietly. Scott Bessent says the budget deficit has a "very good chance" of having peaked, and his department is leaning on a buyback operation north of four billion dollars to try to calm longer-dated yields. CNBC's read this morning is blunt: it hasn't worked yet, and skepticism is building about what else Bessent can actually try against a forty trillion dollar debt pile. That skepticism is showing up in gold, which is rebounding again on debt fears and a weaker dollar. Separately, the U.S. and Canada are reportedly close to a final trade deal, which would head off Trump's threatened fifty percent tariffs that businesses warned could gut their sales. And in Europe, Romania scrambled F-16s to down a drone near a gas project — a reminder the Russia-NATO friction hasn't cooled.

We don't have index or sector levels from the data feed this morning, so I won't guess at numbers I can't back up. What I can tell you is where attention is concentrated: hedge funds, according to MarketWatch, are actually doubling back down on Big Tech after a summer cleanup, even as they dip into healthcare, energy and financials on the side. That's notable positioning ahead of Nvidia's earnings, which MarketWatch frames as a potential circuit-breaker for a stock market that's stalled since the last print. If Jensen Huang's numbers disappoint, there's not a lot else in the calendar to pick up the slack.

On the data front, the July jobs report is still being digested — payrolls unexpectedly fell, but so did the unemployment rate, which is the kind of mixed signal that makes both hawks and doves feel vindicated. That ambiguity is part of why yields have been sticky and why Bessent felt the need to intervene with buybacks in the first place.

Worth your actual attention today: the MarketWatch piece on the forty trillion dollar debt crackdown. It lays out that Washington has options but none are easy, and it's a useful primer for why gold and yields have been moving in tandem lately instead of their usual inverse relationship.
