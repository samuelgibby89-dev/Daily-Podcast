Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia. He resigned after revelations of two extramarital affairs, one of them with a suspected Chinese spy, another with an employee. This isn't a routine succession story. Huang built Nvidia from a graphics chip maker into the company that effectively underwrites the entire AI boom, and for a decade he's been the industry's chief hype man and its most trusted validator. Longtime finance chief Colette Kress is now interim CEO, while new chairman Harvey Jones, on the board since 1993, leads the search for a permanent replacement. Names floated include Arm's Rene Haas, AMD's Lisa Su, and even Apple's Tim Cook. Huang's personal net worth has already dropped twelve percent this week, from about 162 billion dollars to 143 billion.

The fallout is spreading fast. Lawmakers now want Huang to testify about whether one of the relationships compromised chip export decisions, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is facing questions about why he let Nvidia keep selling chips to China. Fortune reports Nvidia also quietly changed how it reported revenue tied to a Singapore partner called Megaspeed last year, which is reviving old questions about Nvidia's past ties to Supermicro and Chinese chip smuggling. None of that is proven wrongdoing, but the timing, with the company already in crisis, is rough. The real economic question is what happens to the so-called neoclouds, the data center operators that built their entire financing pitch around an implicit Nvidia guarantee. That guarantee, as one analyst put it, isn't worth what it was a week ago.

Step back from Nvidia and there's a genuinely good economic story out of Britain. The UK economy grew zero point seven percent in the three months to September, the fastest pace in more than two years, and the BBC's economics editor is flagging that resilience as a real surprise given how gloomy the forecasts were. Meanwhile Metro Bank put up a for-sale sign, though it says it's had no actual approach yet, and Poundland just bought itself ten more days to find a rescue buyer before its deadline hit midnight.

The pattern underneath all of this is concentration risk. Whole sectors, AI infrastructure especially, have been leaning on the judgment and credibility of a handful of individuals and a handful of guarantees. When one person's personal conduct can move a company's market value and ripple through an entire financing ecosystem overnight, that's not a stable foundation. Watch whether Nvidia's customers start demanding contractual protections instead of trusting the brand.

On the calendar: watch for Nvidia's CEO search to produce a shortlist, and keep an eye on how UK growth data feeds into Bank of England thinking.
