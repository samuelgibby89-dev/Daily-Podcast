The American Cancer Society just reversed sixteen years of guidance on prostate cancer. Since 2010, the advice had been cautious screening, weighing against overdiagnosis. Now they're urging routine PSA testing for men between fifty and sixty-nine. This isn't a new trial result — it's a guideline committee re-reading the accumulated evidence and deciding the balance has shifted toward catching more aggressive cancers earlier. Worth knowing if that age range applies to you or someone you know, and worth an actual conversation with a doctor about what a PSA number does and doesn't tell you, rather than treating this as a verdict.

Two bigger-picture developments. A new CRISPR therapy looks set to become the first gene-editing treatment approved that works inside the body — no need to remove, edit, and reinfuse a patient's own cells, which is what made earlier CRISPR treatments so expensive and logistically brutal. That's a real engineering leap, not just a tweak. And a massive international collaboration has mapped the basal ganglia — nearly twenty-six million brain cells profiled across species — building a reference atlas for a brain region tied to Parkinson's, addiction, and a long list of psychiatric conditions. It's a resource, not a treatment, but good maps are how treatments eventually get made.

Also quietly significant: new data on RSV vaccines and monoclonal antibodies show they're cutting hospitalizations from the virus substantially since rollout. That's real-world surveillance data, not a trial promise, which makes it more convincing than it sounds.

Now the one running ahead of itself. Microdosed Ozempic is being sold online as an anti-aging drug — tiny GLP-one doses promising longevity rather than weight loss. The honest answer is there's no solid human evidence for that use at those doses. The drugs' real benefits are well established at prescribed doses for diabetes and obesity; the leap to "live longer on a sliver of a dose" is marketing outrunning data, not a finding anyone should act on.

What to watch: the FDA's decision on that in-body CRISPR therapy, which would be a genuine first. And separately, researchers are racing to reconstruct the early spread of the current Ebola outbreak using phone location data — a reminder that surveillance gaps, not just the virus, are a big part of why it got out of control.
