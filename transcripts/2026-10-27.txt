Good morning. The Fed starts its two-day meeting today, and this one should be boring by design — ninety-three percent of respondents in the CNBC Fed survey expect a hold, and markets agree. The one thing worth watching is Kevin Warsh. He's seen as the hawk in the room, and any language from him about hikes down the road could matter more than the decision itself.

Boeing had a rough morning. Shares sank nine percent after the company said it won't meaningfully ramp up 737 Max production until late 2027 — later than the Street wanted. The longer-term framing, a ten billion dollar free cash flow target by 2029, didn't do much to offset the near-term disappointment. On the other end, JetBlue jumped more than twenty percent after laying out an actual path to profitability, which tells you how starved investors have been for a credible airline turnaround story.

Without index or sector numbers this morning, I'll point you to where the real tension is: AI financing. Bond investors are getting pickier about funding the capital-spending boom — "anything but tech" is the phrase making the rounds — even as Nvidia and AMD said they'll put four billion dollars into Meta to deepen their AI partnership. Meta itself is raising ninety-five billion for infrastructure. So the equity side of this trade still looks hungry. The debt side is where the skepticism is showing up first, and that gap is worth watching closely.

Anthropic's IPO filing is the other thread running through everything. The S-1 landed Monday, a roadshow could start the week of November ninth, and the company could be public the week after. Investors are genuinely split on whether this is a generational listing or priced for perfection in a bubble. Treat the "AI bubble or not" headlines with some skepticism either way — nobody actually knows, and conviction in either direction right now is mostly vibes.

One more backdrop piece worth ten minutes of your day: the growing concern around two trillion dollar deficits colliding with Treasury yields above five percent. That combination raises borrowing costs for everyone — households, companies, the government — and it's becoming a bigger structural story than any single Fed meeting. Worth reading in full if you want the setup for why rates matter beyond this week's decision.
