A new study out today adds a real argument for vaccination that isn't about preventing the infection itself. Researchers found that mice recovering from an acute viral infection had lasting changes to their spleens and weaker immune responses long after symptoms cleared, leaving them more vulnerable to whatever came next. The surprising part: mice that had been vaccinated before infection didn't show this damage at all. It's a mouse study, so don't read this as proof the same hidden damage happens in people. But it's a plausible mechanism worth watching, since it would mean vaccines aren't just blocking one illness, they're protecting the immune system's future capacity.

A few other things worth knowing. Oregon just logged its first H5N1 case in a dairy herd, and a farmworkers' union says human avian flu cases are being undercounted in Washington state. Nothing here suggests a change in how the virus spreads to people, but it's a reminder the dairy outbreak hasn't been contained. Separately, the brain-computer interface company Synchron has paused clinical trials of its second-generation implant. The company says it's not safety-related, more likely a strategic or manufacturing pause, but it's a setback for a field that's been racing to get implants into more patients. And the Department of Health and Human Services is putting up close to five million dollars to fund research into ibogaine, a psychedelic being explored for PTSD and addiction — notable mainly because it signals the federal government is willing to bankroll psychedelic research it wouldn't have touched a few years ago.

Now the one getting ahead of itself: microdosing Ozempic as an anti-aging drug. This is being marketed hard, but there's essentially no clinical evidence that tiny, sub-therapeutic doses of GLP-1 drugs extend lifespan in humans. The hype is running well past the data, and nothing here is a reason to change how these drugs are prescribed for diabetes or obesity, which is where the actual evidence lives.

Worth watching: North Dakota just capped insulin costs for patients, joining a bipartisan trend of states doing this regardless of party. Keep an eye on whether more states follow, since this is becoming one of the few health-cost fights with support on both sides.
