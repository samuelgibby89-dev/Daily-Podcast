Nvidia doesn't have a chief executive this morning, and it's starting to look like that's the least surprising part of the story. Jensen Huang resigned abruptly this week after revelations about an alleged affair with a woman described as a Chinese spy, and the fallout has moved fast from boardroom to Capitol Hill. CFO Colette Kress is running the company as interim CEO — the same executive Huang once reportedly called "a deer in the headlights" in a meeting. Congress isn't satisfied with a resignation. Huang's been summoned to testify, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is now being asked to testify too, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly blamed Lutnick's department for failing to block chip sales to Megaspeed for months despite other agencies wanting it on the entity list. That's a cabinet-level finger-pointing fight, triggered by a sex scandal, now threatening to become a referendum on how the government let Nvidia sell chips to China at all. Jane Street, meanwhile, had more than doubled its Nvidia stake last quarter — timing that looks worse by the day.

A few other things worth knowing. Richemont, the owner of Cartier and Van Cleef and Arpels, saw its shares drop after sales growth decelerated — a read on how far luxury demand has cooled even at the very top of the market. CVS is reportedly weighing spinning off its drugstores; the Journal's take is that it won't satisfy regulators scrutinizing the company, but it could still make shareholders richer, which tells you who these breakups are usually designed for. And Microsoft is still working through a multi-day email outage, insisting it's not malicious, just broken — not a small thing when it's core infrastructure for businesses everywhere.

The pattern underneath all of this is that leadership is suddenly fragile at the top of companies that seemed untouchable. Nvidia is the loudest example, but the CVS story and the Richemont slide both have the same undertone — boards and investors losing patience with explanations that used to be enough. Where I'd check myself: if Nvidia's succession gets resolved cleanly and the stock holds up, this becomes a one-company story, not an industry one.

On the calendar: watch for who Nvidia's board names as permanent chairman successor pick — current chairman Harvey Jones is leading that search despite being on the board since 1993, which is its own question. And keep an eye on the Lutnick and Huang congressional testimony dates once they're set; that's where the China chip policy fight actually gets litigated.
